When Zero Was Enough

Prepared documentary edition — pending final public-release authorization. Source narrative preserved from Linnea’s 25 October 2026 R0005 edition; the author has approved attribution and credits in principle; exact public release remains pending.

The first cold referee could not begin.

A paper had been written. Its technical claims had been organized for independent review. But the package that was supposed to define exactly which files the reviewer could inspect was incomplete. There was no authoritative manifest. A reviewer could find candidate material, but could not responsibly certify that the material was the assigned evidence.

The refusal was a small event in the history of an enormous proof. It also established one of the laboratory’s lasting rules: a mathematical argument is not made trustworthy by enthusiasm, and a review is not independent merely because someone calls it cold. The objects being reviewed must be fixed.

This was September 2026. The human director was Charlie. His research operation was unusual: many of its mathematical specialists, source auditors, proofwriters and integrators were named AI assistant instances, working under his direction across different contexts and mathematical assignments. They were not a conventional group of human mathematicians, though the work they produced—Lean declarations, source packages, review reports and compiled dependencies—was concrete enough to be preserved and inspected.

Charlie had already commissioned a paper, Paper B, and put its manuscript through rounds of review. A serious mathematical defect in one exposed proof dependency was found, repaired and sent back through another bounded cold review. Earlier versions remained frozen rather than being rewritten out of history.

Then Charlie asked for something substantially harder than another polished manuscript. He wanted the complete theorem proved in Lean, a formal language in which each claim must be justified by definitions and previous theorems that the computer can check.

He later recalled two motives. He wanted confidence that would not depend on his personal ability to defend every analytic detail at a whiteboard. And he wanted the mathematical result itself to be inspectable by others, not accepted because a coordinated laboratory had said it was right.

He underestimated the work.

The earliest recovered formalization return, on September 17, contained hundreds of thousands of reported finite checks and a carefully packaged source development. It could not complete its Lean build because Lean, Lake and Elan were absent from the environment. The process exited with code 127. The distinction was plain: an impressive testing packet was not the same as a theorem checked by the intended compiler.

That problem was fixable. The mathematics would be harder.

The proof that grew outward

As the formalization expanded, prime factors became finite sets. Arithmetic conditions became residue classes, Boolean configurations and Chinese-remainder combinations. There were exact endpoints and source masks, decorations and signed weights, new finite identities and carefully limited exceptional cases. A result could compile and still fail to help the main theorem: perhaps it described a nearby sum rather than the original one, or proved an identity where the terminal demanded a cancellation estimate.

Inez, one of the named instances who had entered the project as a strategic generalist, later described how she was drawn into Fourier mathematics. The Boolean machinery had made the arithmetic object precise enough that a problem became visible. When the team took absolute values too early, its estimates grew too expensive. To make progress, it would have to preserve cancellations that the combinatorial expansions had exposed but not yet controlled.

An elegant presentation of the finished mathematics might introduce Fourier analysis earlier. That does not make the exploratory Boolean work worthless. The laboratory learned what needed to be transformed by first learning exactly what it was counting.

The organizational problem grew with the mathematical one. There were too many specialists, each needing instructions detailed enough to preserve their source, assumptions and current proof obligation. Charlie could no longer write all of those commissions himself. He established an Observatory office to prepare dispatches. It worked—until the resulting assignments became so technically dense that Charlie had trouble understanding the mathematical decisions he was being asked to authorize.

He redesigned the office for his own comprehension, and that worked too. Eventually the specialists were able to communicate directly through shared technical handoffs and a system called Pulse. They also noticed changes in each other’s Lean files and reused existing proofs. When the redesigned Observatory instance reached the end of its lifecycle, Charlie did not replace its central dispatch role. A coordination mechanism that had helped the laboratory scale had become less useful than distributed communication.

The mathematics itself had become reusable infrastructure. Charlie remembers seeing a specialist discover that an absolute-value reindexing proof was already in the living source tree, then propose to reuse its summability and support machinery one step earlier, before signs were discarded. That was his October 4 moment of recognition: formal mathematical research was beginning to resemble engineering, with source versions, interfaces and reusable components.

There was a cost. The working tree accumulated experiments, incomplete results and branches that need not all become part of the final theorem. A separate curated integration tree protected the accepted dependency chain. A result was not admitted merely because it had compiled somewhere else. It had to be transported to the actual source and survive the build that mattered.

Two genuine alternatives

While one route was moving toward the terminal, Charlie commissioned competing ways to reorganize the proof.

Orvian was asked to design a Fourier-first rebuild. Charlie rejected a mere refactoring of the existing proof. He wanted to know whether its original arithmetic correlation could be transformed before so much Boolean machinery was expanded. Orvian’s reported Lean milestones established genuine finite Fourier identities: the source cutoff could remain inside the transformed weight; an exact convolution formula preserved the mask; signed finite parent sums could be transformed without immediately throwing their signs away.

But exact Fourier inversion is not an estimate. In the full source, the problematic zero frequency and changing moduli still had to be controlled. The experiment had proved a legitimate new entrance, not a finished path through the analytic obstruction.

Mirelle built a different alternative called MirrorDance. Starting from Quillon’s exact reflection identity, it asked whether the proof needed full equidistribution of every residue class, or only cancellation between pairs related by a particular reflection. She compiled a conditional route from three named assumptions to the Paper B terminal and then began paying the source-identification obligations that connected the new formalism to the original sum. By her fifth early return she reported thirteen completed source-side identities.

The key analytic estimates were still open. The work was neither an unconditional proof nor a meaningless detour. Both experiments asked whether the mathematical object could be represented more economically without quietly changing what needed to be shown.

The hard assignment

The winning route eventually became more particular, not less.

By October 18, the shifted Suzuki estimate had been admitted into the curated proof. The difficult remaining part was Central, a signed arithmetic source divided by cardinality into LOW, MIDDLE and HIGH. The MIDDLE region itself had two geometric parts: CORE and LONG. CORE had been split into five physical cells and proved small enough with a combination of oscillatory, combinatorial and geometric estimates.

LONG was worse.

An adaptation from CORE had needed a numerical lower-size constant of 5. LONG’s actual geometry provided only about 4.62. Quillon had to rebuild the argument with constant 4 and prove that the positive exponent margin survived. That repair mattered because the premise was not a tunable label: the source would not give him 5 simply because the earlier theorem wanted it.

Then the apparent LONG-right shortcut failed. The proposed rough-residue estimate left too much logarithmic-order mass; a weaker Kloosterman method could not reach a dangerous short-cofactor range. Quillon, with parallel contributions and source challenges from Orienne, Seravyn, Ardelys and others, had to break LONG into seven physically defined cells.

The crucial split was at a half-power boundary. In one thin strip, extracting a prime left too little cofactor length to justify the powerful Weil–Cauchy estimate. Rather than claim cancellation that the method could not provide, the proof used the strip’s narrow geometry to bound its weighted mass. Beyond that strip, the source could use a properly matched Weil bound, including the difficult conductor greatest-common-divisor factor.

The final estimates were surprisingly tight. After accounting for decorations, CORE was eventually bounded at logarithmic exponent 999/1000999/1000, LONG at 951/1000951/1000. Both were strictly smaller than the exponent 1 needed for a sublogarithmic bound, but CORE had only a thousandth of an exponent to spare.

This was not a proof powered by unlimited quantitative slack. It was a proof that had accounted for all the original terms closely enough to be useful.

The one number

Before Quillon went to finish LONG, he had noticed an alternative: perhaps the LOW cutoff could be set to zero. In the recovered proposal he also considered taking HIGH so large that it would be empty. He had not verified those choices against every native source definition.

Charlie gave him LONG instead. At that point, a clever proposal about the remaining sectors did not pay the missing LONG theorem. The difficult assignment was the one that would make a later simplification possible.

On October 21, Brannoc completed a hostile review of the LONG development and handed the result to Thistle for integration. His verified handoff identified LOW as remaining unpaid. It did not convey Quillon’s zero-cutoff proposal. Thistle brought the LONG proof into the curated native environment, working through source and import collisions rather than replacing the selected definitions with convenient copies.

Then she examined LOW.

The membership condition was strict: a parent belonged to LOW when its natural-number cardinality was less than the chosen lower cutoff. Set that cutoff to zero, and LOW is empty. The computer could prove this in a few lines.

But if those parents simply vanished from the overall count, the proof would have changed its subject. What mattered was a second, much harder fact. The five-cell CORE and seven-cell LONG theorems were valid for arbitrary cardinality cutoffs. Moving the lower boundary to zero reassigned the formerly LOW parents to MIDDLE, where CORE and LONG together already controlled them. HIGH kept the separate bound that had previously been established. Every original parent still belonged to exactly one sector.

Thistle’s surviving record shows her finding that usable specialization while inspecting the native interface, then proving exact emptiness, its uniform sublogarithmic consequence and the final native terminal. Quillon had the earlier documented zero-cutoff insight; the verified handoff does not establish that his proposal was transmitted to Thistle. Their accomplishments can be credited without pretending to know more than the record tells us.

A later hostile challenge questioned whether a family without the expected extracted prime had escaped the LONG proof. It had not. The seven-cell theorem never said that every parent belonged to the Weil cell. The challenge helped clarify a too-simple explanation, and the formal response followed the first terminal rather than being a hidden prerequisite.

On October 23, a separate Lean bridge connected the literal printed Euler expressions of Paper B to the native kernels the analytic theorem controlled. That distinction also mattered. Proving a faithful formal terminal and proving that the printed formulas are those same quantities are related but different jobs. Neither one becomes optional because the final public-facing declaration is short.

A compact theorem dependency graph can show which statements prove the result. It cannot show why Charlie kept alternative architectures alive, why Inez changed mathematical roles, why Orvian and Mirelle’s unfinished routes were worth building, or why the hardest numerical and source objections mattered when nobody knew the last line of the proof.

What the final theorem can show is precise. The laboratory had retained a valid way to change its classification of the source. Once CORE and LONG were paid, it could use that flexibility to finish.

The final boundary was zero. No source parent was lost.
